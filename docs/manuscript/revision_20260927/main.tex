\begin{abstract}
Ventricular septal defect (VSD) is one of the most common congenital heart defect which usually be closed using a closure procedure based on transcatheter. Although closure is usually successful, individual heart characteristics and chamber remodelling can cause a problem in pre-surgery planning, which can make the results become unpredictable. Computer modelling based on Lumped Parameter Model (LPM) has been used in wide range of congenital heart defects problem. However, relatively small amount of studies have build it directly for VSD patients, let alone on a patient-specifics model which persists on its pre and post closure surgery prediction. We presents a zero dimensional lumped-parameter framework in which closure represented by eliminating shunt pathway between the defect combined with pediatric scaling, multi-start calibration, and global sensitivity analisis to built a patient specific pediatric VSD model. The model was aimed to reproduced the primary VSD hemodynamics target within 10\% range in every run, with rejection and acceptance based on its 9 determined primary metrics. Based on the three run reproducibility, our model reached the best accuracy at 5.46\% with average run producing 6.65\%. After model reached steady state, Global Sensitivity Analysis was done and determine the most significant parameters as systemic peripheral resistance (R.SC), systemic venous compliance (C.SVEN), and VSD discharge coefficient (vsd.Cd). The seeded run from pre closure surgery was used to identify and determine the predictive post-closure run, which reached 24.2\% average error, with only 3 of 13 targets falling within the 10\% range. This study shows that the models Lumped Parameter Model integrated with weight-based scaling and Global Sensitivity Analysis can be used on a one continous predictive pipeline, which lays the necessary foundation on creating towards a digital twins in managing VSD in both pediatrics and adults.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Ventricular septal defect, lumped-parameter model, patient-specific modeling, global sensitivity analysis, parameter identifiability, congenital heart disease

\section{Introduction}

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is one of the most common birth defects, with a global prevalence estimated to be 9.41 per 1,000 live births during 2010--2017, 10\% higher than the previous 15 years~\cite{Liu2019}. Ventricular septal defect (VSD) is one of the most common types of CHD with an incidence of 3.07 per 1,000 live births and accounting for 35.6\% of all CHD cases worldwide~\cite{Liu2019,vanderLinde2011}. Asia has reported the highest cases of CHD of any region at 9.34 per 1000 live births with mild lesions such as VSD being more frequent than elsewhere~\cite{Liu2019}. In Indonesia alone, 43,200 infants are born each year with CHD and early detection is limited outside large population centers~\cite{Marwali2021}. VSD creates an abnormal channel between the ventricles, which causes the blood to be shunted across the chambers. The shunt itself depended on its defect size, interventricular pressure gradient, and the ratio of pulmonary to systemic vascular resistance~\cite{Sommer2008,Rao2024}. A left-to-right shunt increases the pulmonary blood flow and left-heart volume loading when it becomes hemodynamically significant, which, if left untreated, could lead to heart failure and pulmonary vascular remodeling~\cite{Sommer2008,Arvanitaki2022}. While many small defects close spontaneously during early childhood, many defects with larger size and particular anatomical subtypes fail to close, leaving a group of children who require closure~\cite{Zhang2015Spontaneous,Li2019Spontaneous}.

Transcatheter closure is often preferred because it avoids cardiopulmonary bypass in anatomically suitable cases~\cite{Turner2022,Ali_Hassan_2019}. However, the procedure itself does not guarantee a successful hemodynamic course, as 36.4\% of children still have residual shunting immediately after transcatheter closure, and 9.8\% still have residual shunting at follow-up~\cite{Vuran2026}. Another study reported 3.1\% of children requiring unplanned surgery after device closure due to new or worsening aortic regurgitation~\cite{Yang2021Surgery}. Those residual shunts and worsening aortic regurgitation are associated with the defect diameter and the young age of the patient~\cite{Chin2024}, while low body weight is associated with adverse events after surgical closure~\cite{Abdelrehim2022}, which created patient-specific risk factors. These findings created a need for pre-procedural identification of patients whose conditions will respond unfavorably to closure in order to reduce the number of follow-up interventions.

While echocardiography could determine the pre-surgery condition, its operation is operator-dependent and prone to diagnostic error in young children~\cite{Benavidez2008}. At the same time, cardiac catheterization, which is considered the gold standard, captures only a single physiological state~\cite{Feltes2011}. Neither modality predicts how the pressure, flows, and chambers of patients will react once the shunt is removed. A quantitative and predictive patient-specific model would therefore be useful in predicting the hemodynamic outcome of closure before the procedure. Patient-specific computational models combine clinical measurements with mathematical modeling to reconstruct the hemodynamic process based on direct measurements taken from patients, allowing for the simulation of states that have yet to occur~\cite{Taylor2009}. In cardiology, such models have been used to plan surgical intervention or evaluate the disease progression~\cite{Marsden2015,Pennati2013}. The models also form the core for emerging digital twins, in which a calibrated model is continually improved and measured by the patient's own data to support individual decisions~\cite{Katsoulakis2024}. Such models are often built with three-dimensional computational fluid dynamics, but their computational cost limits repeated use~\cite{Pennati2013}. Zero-dimensional lumped-parameter models (LPMs) offer an alternative at a fraction of the cost~\cite{Shi2011}.

LPMs have been applied across congenital and pediatric conditions. Shimizu et al.\ developed LPM models across several CHD configurations and successfully reproduced the characteristics of hemodynamics of each CHD impact to the body~\cite{Shimizu2018}, while Schiavazzi et al.\ estimated the parameters of a closed-loop circulation under measurement uncertainty~\cite{Schiavazzi2017}. LPM is also used to determine the pressure and flow of Fontan circulations in research done by Horio et al.~\cite{Horio2024}. Bozkurt developed a model that simulates cardiac function and chamber dimensions in adults and children and optimizes the parameters to reproduce the variables used in the models~\cite{Bozkurt2019,Bozkurt2022}. For specific VSD models, Lee and Jung coupled a three-dimensional heart to lumped circulation to reproduce the physiological flow of VSD-impacted hearts' circulations~\cite{Lee2015}, while Ferrero et al.\ used a 0D framework to interpret the catheterization data from patients with intracardiac shunts~\cite{Ferrero2026Shunts}. Most recently, Colebank et al.\ combined an eight-compartment LPM with age-dependent parameters to simulate small, medium, and large VSD from birth to two years of age~\cite{Colebank2026}.

Despite this progress, studies that model the computational simulation of VSD remain scarce~\cite{Colebank2026}, and to our knowledge, few studies have calibrated a VSD model to individual children or validated it against their clinical measurements. While adapting the models may appear straightforward, several challenges persist that complicate the adaptation process. First, the adult reference parameters cannot be directly used on a small child. The model needs to be coupled with either weight or body surface area to scale and reproduce the age-related changes from infancy to adolescence~\cite{zhang2019multiscale,Lundquist2025}. Second, patient-specific models usually contain needed parameters that available clinical measurements could not have, which makes the model's accuracy constrained~\cite{Schiavazzi2017}. Global sensitivity analysis can address this by identifying the most influential parameters, allowing less influential ones to be fixed and reducing the number to be estimated~\cite{Saltelli2010,vanOsta2020}. These approaches, however, have not been combined into a single workflow, and the identifiability of the parameters is rarely reported, which makes it difficult to create a post-closure prediction based on available parameters. The aim of this study is to develop a patient-specific LPM of pediatric VSD, which is calibrated to pre-closure clinical data and used to predict the post-closure circulation of the same patient. Specifically, we have four main goals: (i) we want to adapt a weight-based scaling to the models; (ii) we want to select the calibration parameters based on variance-based global sensitivity analysis and estimate them by multi-start optimization and determine their reproducibility by different seeded runs; (iii) we want to audit the identifiability of each calibrated parameter set; and (iv) we want to evaluate the pre- and post-closure prediction against catheterization and echocardiography measurements acquired from clinical records. To our knowledge, this is the first workflow that calibrates a pediatric VSD model to a child's pre-closure measurements and then tests its prediction against the same child's post-closure measurements, while reporting both the reproducibility and the identifiability of the calibrated parameters.

\section{Materials and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} summarizes the workflow: the clinical record, pediatric scaling and clinical seeding, the sensitivity screen, calibration, the post-closure prediction and the measurement-noise simulation.

\subsection{Study population}
\label{sec:study_population}
This single-patient case study used clinical data collected at the National Center for Women and Children's Health, RSAB Harapan Kita, Jakarta, Indonesia, between December 2025 and June 2026. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of RSAB Harapan Kita (approval no.\ DP.04.03/D.XXII/4749/2025). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient's legal guardian. Data were de-identified and the patient is referred to as P1.

Inclusion criteria were: (i) age above three months; (ii) VSD diagnosed by a pediatric cardiologist on clinical assessment and echocardiography; (iii) defect diameter $\ge 2$~mm; and (iv) scheduled transcatheter closure with anthropometric, echocardiographic and catheterization measurements sufficient to initialize and evaluate a patient-specific model. Exclusion criteria were: (i) missing or invalid core hemodynamic measurements; (ii) an additional severe cardiovascular abnormality expected to dominate the hemodynamics; and (iii) previous VSD closure or other corrective cardiac intervention. P1 was the first enrolled patient with a complete pre- and post-closure record.

\subsection{Clinical data}
\label{sec:data}
Clinical data were obtained from the catheterization protocol form and the medical record at RSAB Harapan Kita and included demographic, anthropometric, hemodynamic and cardiac structural measurements (Table~\ref{tab:patient_data}). Abbreviations used throughout: LV, RV, LA and RA, left and right ventricle and atrium; PA, pulmonary artery; RFA, right femoral artery; DAo, descending aorta; SVR, systemic vascular resistance; EDV, ESV and EF, end-diastolic volume, end-systolic volume and ejection fraction. The defect diameter was measured on echocardiography at both ventricular sides (5.445~mm on the left-ventricular side and 3.665~mm on the right-ventricular side); the right-ventricular-side diameter, which is the restrictive orifice, was used as the model input ($D_{\mathrm{VSD}} = 3.665$~mm). Heart rate was recorded separately for each state (119~bpm before and 108~bpm after closure) and used as a model input in each state.

Mean arterial pressure was computed with the formula of the catheterization protocol, $\overline{P} = (P_{\mathrm{sys}} + 2P_{\mathrm{dia}})/3$, from the RFA catheter readings. This gives 71.33~mmHg before and 75.00~mmHg after closure. Pulmonary flow $Q_p = 4.087$~L/min and $Q_p/Q_s = 1.194$ were measured by the Fick method during catheterization; systemic flow (cardiac output) and shunt flow were derived from them as $Q_s = Q_p/(Q_p/Q_s) = 3.423$~L/min and $Q_{\mathrm{shunt}} = Q_p - Q_s = 0.664$~L/min. Pulmonary and systemic vascular resistance were not recorded on the protocol form.

After device deployment, invasive pressures were recorded by catheter approximately five minutes after closure. Echocardiography was performed approximately one day later and provided the post-closure volumes: left-ventricular volumes by the biplane method and right-ventricular volumes by three-dimensional echocardiography. The model treats these volumes as belonging to the same post-closure state as the catheter pressures, which assumes that the volumes did not change between five minutes and one day after closure. A second record of left-ventricular volumes exists on the protocol form (LVEDV/LVESV 32.0/23.6~mL); both records are evaluated and the medical-record values are the primary comparison.

\subsection{Mathematical model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsubsection{Structure}
The circulation is a closed-loop lumped-parameter network adapted from the intracardiac-shunt architecture of Ferrero et al.~\cite{Ferrero2026Shunts} (Figure~\ref{fig:schematic}): four time-varying elastance chambers, a systemic circuit (arterial, capillary and venous compartments), a pulmonary circuit (arterial compartment, capillary compartment with parallel oxygenated and non-oxygenated branches, venous compartment), four valves, and an interventricular shunt. Vascular compartments use resistance--compliance--inertance elements~\cite{Shi2011}. The state vector has 14 entries,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}\mathbf{X} = \big[ & V_{\mathrm{RA}}, V_{\mathrm{RV}}, V_{\mathrm{LA}}, V_{\mathrm{LV}}, V_{\mathrm{SAR}}, Q_{\mathrm{SAR}}, V_{\mathrm{SC}}, \\
  & V_{\mathrm{SVEN}}, Q_{\mathrm{SVEN}}, V_{\mathrm{PAR}}, Q_{\mathrm{PAR}}, P_{\mathrm{PC}}, V_{\mathrm{PVEN}}, Q_{\mathrm{PVEN}} \big]^{\top},\end{split}
\label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
with volumes $V$ (mL), flows $Q$ (mL/s) and the pulmonary capillary pressure $P_{\mathrm{PC}}$ (mmHg). Chamber volumes obey
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\dot{V}_{\mathrm{RA}} &= Q_{\mathrm{SVEN}} - Q_{\mathrm{TV}}, &
\dot{V}_{\mathrm{RV}} &= Q_{\mathrm{TV}} + Q_{\mathrm{VSD}} - Q_{\mathrm{PV}}, \\
\dot{V}_{\mathrm{LA}} &= Q_{\mathrm{PVEN}} - Q_{\mathrm{MV}}, &
\dot{V}_{\mathrm{LV}} &= Q_{\mathrm{MV}} - Q_{\mathrm{AV}} - Q_{\mathrm{VSD}},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:chambers}
\end{equation}
and the vascular mass and momentum balances are given in Appendix~\ref{app:circuit}. Nominal adult reference values are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:nominal_parameters}.

\subsubsection{Cardiac chambers}
Chamber pressure follows the time-varying elastance concept~\cite{Suga1974,Walley2016},
\begin{equation}
P_{ch}(t) = E_{ch}(t)\left(V_{ch}(t) - V_{0,ch}\right), \qquad
E_{ch}(t) = E_{\mathrm{B},ch} + E_{\mathrm{A},ch}\, e_{ch}(t),
\label{eq:elastance}
\end{equation}
with unstressed volume $V_{0,ch}$, passive elastance $E_{\mathrm{B},ch}$, active amplitude $E_{\mathrm{A},ch}$ and normalized activation $e_{ch}(t)\in[0,1]$. Ventricular activation over the cardiac phase $\phi = \mathrm{mod}(t, T_{\mathrm{HB}})$, $T_{\mathrm{HB}} = 60/\mathrm{HR}$, is the piecewise cosine of Colebank et al.~\cite{Colebank2026} and Ferrero et al.~\cite{Ferrero2026Shunts},
\begin{equation}
e_v(\phi) =
\begin{cases}
\tfrac{1}{2}\left[1-\cos\left(\pi\phi/T_{vc}\right)\right], & 0 \le \phi \le T_{vc},\\[3pt]
\tfrac{1}{2}\left[1+\cos\left(\pi(\phi-T_{vc})/T_{vr}\right)\right], & T_{vc} < \phi \le T_{vc}+T_{vr},\\[3pt]
0, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:activation}
\end{equation}
where $T_{vc}$ and $T_{vr}$ are the contraction and relaxation durations, specified as fractions of $T_{\mathrm{HB}}$ so that the timing rescales with heart rate. Atrial activation uses the same form evaluated at $\tau = \mathrm{mod}(\phi - t_{ac}, T_{\mathrm{HB}})$, with $t_{ac}$ the onset of atrial contraction in late diastole.

\FloatBarrier
\subsubsection{Valves and interventricular shunt}
\label{sec:valves}
Ideal diodes introduce discontinuities that degrade stiff integration. Each valve is therefore a smooth gate between an open and a closed resistance, the continuous-switch approach used in closed-loop lumped-parameter models~\cite{Shi2011}; the hyperbolic-tangent form below is the implementation adopted in this work,
\begin{equation}
g = \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\tanh\!\left(\frac{\Delta P}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}}}\right), \qquad
Q_{\mathrm{valve}} = \Delta P\left(\frac{g}{R_{\mathrm{open}}} + \frac{1-g}{R_{\mathrm{closed}}}\right),
\label{eq:valve}
\end{equation}
with transvalvular gradient $\Delta P$, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{v}} = 0.5$~mmHg, $R_{\mathrm{open}} = 4.0\times10^{-3}$ and $R_{\mathrm{closed}} = 9.42\times10^{4}$~mmHg\,s/mL.

Shunt flow uses the Gorlin orifice relation~\cite{Gorlin1951}. The square-root law is unmodified; the sign-preserving factor that makes it differentiable through zero gradient is introduced here,
\begin{equation}
Q_{\mathrm{VSD}} = 10^{6}\, C_d\, A_{\mathrm{VSD}} \sqrt{\frac{2\,\gamma\,\lvert\Delta P_{\mathrm{v}}\rvert}{\rho}}\;
\underbrace{\frac{\Delta P_{\mathrm{v}}}{\sqrt{\Delta P_{\mathrm{v}}^{2} + \varepsilon_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}}}}_{\text{smoothing (this work)}},
\qquad A_{\mathrm{VSD}} = \pi\left(\frac{D_{\mathrm{VSD}}}{2}\right)^{2},
\label{eq:orifice}
\end{equation}
where $Q_{\mathrm{VSD}}$ is in mL/s, $A_{\mathrm{VSD}}$ in m$^2$ (the factor $10^{6}$ converts m$^3$/s to mL/s), $\Delta P_{\mathrm{v}} = P_{\mathrm{LV}} - P_{\mathrm{RV}}$ in mmHg (positive left-to-right), $\gamma = 133.322$~Pa/mmHg, $\rho = 1060$~kg/m$^3$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{s}} = 0.1$~mmHg. The discharge coefficient $C_d$ lumps defect shape and flow contraction and is the only shunt parameter calibrated. Closure sets $A_{\mathrm{VSD}} = 0$, which makes $Q_{\mathrm{VSD}} = 0$ exactly in Eq.~(\ref{eq:orifice}); this was checked numerically before every post-closure simulation.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Pediatric scaling and initialization}

\subsubsection{Allometric scaling}
Adult reference parameters were scaled by the weight-based allometric law of Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2019multiscale},
\begin{equation}
\theta_i = \theta_{i,\mathrm{ref}} \left(\frac{W_{\mathrm{p}}}{W_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^{\alpha_i},
\label{eq:scaling}
\end{equation}
with $W_{\mathrm{ref}} = 70$~kg, $W_{\mathrm{p}} = 13.4$~kg and the class exponents of Table~\ref{tab:exponents}. Zhang et al.\ report no exponent for inertance; $\alpha = -1$ was adopted from the mechanical-similarity argument of Lundquist et al.~\cite{Lundquist2025} and is the only exponent not taken from~\cite{zhang2019multiscale}. The age-dependent maturation correction of systemic and pulmonary resistance described by Colebank et al.~\cite{Colebank2026} is implemented; at the age of P1 (1158 days) both correction factors equal 1.000, so it has no effect here. The scaled heart rate is replaced by the measured heart rate of each state. No alternative scaling law was applied.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Numerical solution}
The system was integrated with \texttt{ode15s}~\cite{Shampine1997} in MATLAB R2025a (relative tolerance $10^{-5}$, absolute tolerance $10^{-6}$, maximum step $T_{\mathrm{HB}}/20$). The solver advanced the model in batches of five cardiac cycles and compared the peak values of the states between consecutive batches. Periodic steady state was declared when the volume states changed by less than 0.1~mL, the pulmonary capillary pressure by less than 0.1~mmHg, and the flow states by less than 0.5\% of their peak, with a maximum of 80 cycles; outputs were computed from two further cycles. The scaled baseline, the calibrated pre-closure state and the post-closure prediction reached steady state; the exported post-closure cycle closes to within 0.03~mL in every volume state.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Global sensitivity analysis}
\label{sec:gsa}
Variance-based global sensitivity analysis~\cite{Sobol2001,Saltelli2010}, following recommendations for cardiovascular models~\cite{Eck2016}, ranked the parameters by their influence on the model outputs. Sparse polynomial chaos expansions (PCE)~\cite{Blatman2011} were fitted in UQLab~2.2~\cite{Marelli2014} (least-angle regression, degree 1--3, hyperbolic truncation $q = 0.75$) to one shared Halton design of $N = 128$ points over $d = 21$ independent uniform inputs. Input ranges were $[0.4,\,2.5]\,x_0$ for resistances, $[0.5,\,2.0]\,x_0$ for compliances and elastances, $[0.6,\,1.7]\,x_0$ for unstressed volumes and $[0.8,\,1.2]\,x_0$ for $C_d$. Total-order Sobol' indices were obtained analytically from the expansion coefficients~\cite{Sudret2008},
\begin{equation}
S_{T_i} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\mathbb{E}\left[Y \mid \mathbf{X}_{\sim i}\right]\right)}{\mathrm{Var}(Y)},
\label{eq:sobol}
\end{equation}
for 25 outputs. Of the 21 inputs, 17 form the 14 calibration candidates: $R_{\mathrm{SAR}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{SC}}$ form one systemic resistance scale, and $R_{\mathrm{PAR}}$, $R_{\mathrm{PCOX}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{PVEN}}$ one pulmonary resistance scale, each group taking the largest index of its members. The other four inputs, the venous compliances $C_{\mathrm{SVEN}}$ and $C_{\mathrm{PVEN}}$ and the atrial unstressed volumes $V_{0,\mathrm{LA}}$ and $V_{0,\mathrm{RA}}$, are not in the calibration parameter set of the code, which was fixed before the runs; they were screened to measure their influence and held at their scaled values. A candidate was retained for calibration if $S_{T} \ge 0.10$ for at least one of the nine fitted targets (Section~\ref{sec:comparison}); the screen fixes the set of fitted parameters and no later stage adds a parameter. To keep the design affordable, each of the 128 training simulations was run for at most 10 cycles with a steady-state tolerance of 1.0~mmHg and 1.0~mL; outputs were taken from cycles 9--10. The screen was repeated at the calibrated operating point with the same relative input ranges, so the absolute range sampled for each parameter moved with its calibrated value. A 128-point design supports ranking rather than precise index values, and the indices are reported as a screening result.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}

\subsubsection{Objective function}
Calibration minimized
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
J(\mathbf{x}) = {} & \sum_{k \in \mathcal{H}^{*}} w_k\left(\frac{\varepsilon_k}{0.05}\right)^{2}
+ \sum_{k \in \mathcal{S}} w_k\left(\frac{\varepsilon_k}{0.10}\right)^{2}
+ J_{\mathrm{sys}} + J_{\mathrm{shape}} \\
& + \lambda_{\mathrm{g}} \sum_{k \in \mathcal{G}} \left[\frac{\max\left(0,\,\lvert\varepsilon_k\rvert - 0.10\right)}{0.10}\right]^{2}
+ \lambda_{\mathrm{p}}\sum_{j} c_j \ln^{2}\!\left(\frac{x_j}{x_j^{\mathrm{s}}}\right) + J_{\mathrm{b}} + J_{\mathrm{r}},
\end{split}
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
with the signed relative error $\varepsilon_k = (y_k(\mathbf{x}) - y_k^{\mathrm{clin}})/y_k^{\mathrm{clin}}$. The fitted targets fall into two weighting groups. $\mathcal{H}^{*}$ contains mean PA pressure and $Q_p/Q_s$, normalized by 0.05; mean RA pressure, mean arterial pressure and $Q_s$, which describe one systemic load, enter together through the coupled term $J_{\mathrm{sys}}$, so that one systemic mismatch is not counted three times. $\mathcal{S}$ contains systolic and diastolic PA and RFA pressure and shunt flow, normalized by 0.10. $J_{\mathrm{shape}}$ fits the systemic and pulmonary pulse pressures and the shunt flow implied by $Q_s(Q_p/Q_s - 1)$. The fourth term ($\lambda_{\mathrm{g}} = 20$) adds a quadratic penalty to any of the nine fitted targets $\mathcal{G}$ whose relative error exceeds 10\%, and is zero otherwise. The prior term penalizes drift from the scaled value $x_j^{\mathrm{s}}$ in proportion to prior confidence $c_j$ ($\lambda_{\mathrm{p}} = 0.375$), $J_{\mathrm{b}}$ penalizes parameters within 10\% of a bound ($\lambda = 17$), and $J_{\mathrm{r}}$ penalizes outputs outside pediatric physiological ranges and non-periodic simulations. The target weights $w_k$ and all term weights are listed in Supplementary Table~S3. Equation~(\ref{eq:objective}) is specific to this work.

\subsubsection{Staged multi-start optimization}
For each starting point, six bound-constrained subproblems were solved in sequence with \texttt{fmincon}: vascular parameters and $C_d$ (stage A), chamber parameters (stage B), up to five retained parameters jointly (stage C), a systemic-output refinement (stage D), a refinement with stronger prior and bound penalties ($\lambda_{\mathrm{p}} = 2.25$, $\lambda = 120$; stage E), and a final refinement on the nine fitted targets (stage F). Stages A--E used the interior-point algorithm and stage F used sequential quadratic programming; each stage was limited to 300 function evaluations and 40 iterations. A refinement was kept only if it increased neither the RMSE of relative errors (Eq.~\ref{eq:rmse}) nor the number of fitted targets with a relative error above 10\%. Six starting points were used: the clinically seeded vector and five scrambled Sobol' points inside the bounds, generated with the multi-start seed 101, which was fixed in advance. The result of one starting point was selected in three ordered steps: fewest of the five mean-pressure and flow targets with a relative error above 10\%, then fewest of the nine fitted targets with a relative error above 10\%, then lowest RMSE. The 10\% value in the objective and in this selection rule is an internal setting of the optimizer; the Results report the relative errors themselves and apply no threshold. Repeating the complete pipeline with the seeds 202 and 303 returned the identical parameter set; these repeats are reported in Supplementary Section~S1.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Post-closure prediction}
\label{sec:post_methods}
The post-closure state is a prediction, not a fit. The calibrated pre-closure parameter set was applied unchanged with the defect closed ($A_{\mathrm{VSD}} = 0$) and the heart rate set to its measured post-closure value (108~bpm); no parameter was refitted and no post-closure measurement influenced calibration. Differences from the withheld measurements are therefore attributable to the model together with its pre-closure parameters.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Comparison between model and measurements}
\label{sec:comparison}
\label{sec:statistics}
Every measured quantity is reported with its measured value, the model value and the signed relative error $\varepsilon_k$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:objective}), in the same order and with the same symbols before and after closure. Where a measurement uncertainty is declared, the standardized residual $z_k = (y_k^{\mathrm{model}} - y_k^{\mathrm{clin}})/\sigma_k$ is also given, with $\sigma_k$ the declared one-standard-deviation uncertainty: 5\% of the measured value for catheter RA and PA pressures, mean arterial pressure and $Q_p/Q_s$; 10\% for systolic and diastolic systemic pressure and for ventricular volumes and ejection fractions; 0.50~L/min (14.6\%) for pre-closure $Q_s$; 5\% for post-closure $Q_s$; and 20\% for derived quantities (shunt flow, SVR).

The nine fitted targets are mean RA pressure, mean, systolic and diastolic PA pressure, mean, systolic and diastolic RFA pressure, $Q_s$ and $Q_p/Q_s$. The same nine quantities are summarized in both states by the root-mean-square of their relative errors,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{9}\sum_{k=1}^{9}\varepsilon_k^{2}},
\label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}
and by $\chi^2 = \sum_k z_k^2$, given per observation ($\chi^2/N$) and, before closure, per degree of freedom ($N - p$). Shunt flow is fitted but excluded from these summaries because it is the difference of two fitted flows. After closure, $\chi^2/N$ is also given over all 16 quantities with a declared uncertainty. No acceptance threshold is applied to any result.

Following Lechuga et al.~\cite{Lechuga2026}, agreement across all compared quantities was also summarized in parity plots of model against measurement, with the RMSE in physical units and, for groups of at least five points, the coefficient of determination $R^2$. Ventricular pressure--volume loops were characterized by end-diastolic volume (maximum volume), end-systolic volume (minimum volume), stroke volume, ejection fraction, end-diastolic pressure (pressure at maximum volume), end-systolic pressure (pressure at the maximum of $P/V$), peak pressure and stroke work, $\mathrm{SW} = \oint P\,dV$. End-systolic pressure is taken at the maximum of $P/V$ because, with an open defect, the left ventricle keeps emptying into the right ventricle during relaxation and its minimum volume occurs after pressure has fallen. Because one patient was studied, no between-patient statistics were computed. The practical identifiability of the calibrated parameter set is reported in Supplementary Section~S2.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Measurement-noise simulation}
\label{sec:noise_methods}
The effect of measurement uncertainty on the calibrated parameters and on the post-closure prediction was assessed by Monte Carlo simulation. In each of $M = 50$ replicates, every directly measured pre-closure target was perturbed independently by zero-mean Gaussian noise with standard deviation equal to its declared uncertainty $\sigma_k$ (Section~\ref{sec:comparison}): mean RA pressure (0.25~mmHg), mean, systolic and diastolic PA pressure (0.75, 1.0 and 0.5~mmHg), systolic and diastolic RFA pressure (10 and 5.7~mmHg), $Q_s$ (0.50~L/min) and $Q_p/Q_s$ (0.060). Quantities that the clinical record derives from these measurements were recomputed from the perturbed values with the same formulas: mean arterial pressure as $(P_{\mathrm{sys}} + 2P_{\mathrm{dia}})/3$ and shunt flow as $Q_s(Q_p/Q_s - 1)$. A draw was redrawn only if it was physically impossible (a non-positive value, diastolic above systolic pressure, mean PA pressure outside its systolic--diastolic range, or $Q_p/Q_s \le 1$). Model inputs (age, weight, height, heart rate, defect diameter) and all post-closure measurements were not perturbed. The noise draws used a fixed seed (20260926).

Each perturbed record was recalibrated with the procedure of Section~\ref{sec:calibration} unchanged: the same clinical seeding, objective, six starting points and multi-start seed. The fitted parameter set was held at the seven parameters selected by the screen of Section~\ref{sec:gsa}, so that only the measurements differ between replicates. The recalibrated parameters were then applied with the defect closed, as in Section~\ref{sec:post_methods}, and the prediction was compared with the unperturbed post-closure record. For each calibrated parameter and each predicted quantity we report the mean, standard deviation, coefficient of variation (CV) and 2.5th--97.5th percentile interval across replicates, beside the measured value. Every replicate is reported, including replicates whose calibration could not start.

\subsection{Implementation}
The model, calibration and analyses were implemented in MATLAB R2025a (MathWorks, Natick, MA) with UQLab~2.2~\cite{Marelli2014}; figures were drawn in R~4.5.2 with ggplot2. One script (\texttt{run\_all.m}) reproduces the pre-closure pipeline, the post-closure prediction and every exported table, and a second (\texttt{run\_noise\_simulation.m}) reproduces the measurement-noise simulation.

\subsection{Sensitivity analysis}
\label{sec:results_gsa}
The screen retained 7 of the 14 candidate parameters (Figure~\ref{fig:gsa}A). Ranked by their largest total-order index over the nine fitted targets, the retained parameters were the systemic resistance scale ($S_T = 0.70$), the discharge coefficient $C_d$ (0.51), the passive right-ventricular elastance $E_{\mathrm{B,RV}}$ (0.48), the pulmonary resistance scale (0.40), the pulmonary arterial compliance $C_{\mathrm{PAR}}$ (0.39), the systemic arterial compliance $C_{\mathrm{SAR}}$ (0.12) and the systemic venous resistance $R_{\mathrm{SVEN}}$ (0.10). The other seven candidates stayed below 0.10 for every fitted target: $E_{\mathrm{A,RV}}$ (0.080), $E_{\mathrm{B,LV}}$ (0.073), $E_{\mathrm{A,LV}}$ (0.031), $E_{\mathrm{A,RA}}$ (0.006), $E_{\mathrm{A,LA}}$ (0.005), $V_{0,\mathrm{RV}}$ (0.004) and $V_{0,\mathrm{LV}}$ (0.001).

Each fitted target was controlled by one or two parameters (Figure~\ref{fig:gsa}B). The systemic resistance scale controlled diastolic RFA pressure ($S_T = 0.70$), mean arterial pressure (0.58), systolic RFA pressure (0.43), $Q_p/Q_s$ (0.41) and systemic flow (0.33). $C_d$ controlled $Q_p/Q_s$ (0.51). $E_{\mathrm{B,RV}}$ controlled mean RA pressure (0.48). The pulmonary resistance scale controlled diastolic and mean PA pressure (0.40 and 0.30), and $C_{\mathrm{PAR}}$ controlled systolic PA pressure (0.39). At the calibrated operating point (Supplementary Figure~S2), the dominant parameter was the same for eight of the nine targets. For systolic PA pressure, the pulmonary resistance scale became dominant ($S_T = 0.25$) and $C_{\mathrm{PAR}}$ fell to 0.06; calibration lowered $C_{\mathrm{PAR}}$ from its seed of 3.43 to 1.02~mL/mmHg, which narrows the absolute range sampled around it.

The parameters left out by the screen controlled the chamber outputs that have no pre-closure measurement: $E_{\mathrm{A,LV}}$ controlled LVESV ($S_T = 0.59$) and LVEF (0.63), and $E_{\mathrm{A,RV}}$ controlled RVESV (0.71) and RVEF (0.76). Of the four screened inputs that are not calibration candidates, $C_{\mathrm{SVEN}}$ exceeded 0.10 for five fitted targets (largest 0.14, diastolic PA pressure) and reached 0.18 for systolic PA pressure at the calibrated operating point; $C_{\mathrm{PVEN}}$ stayed below 0.03 at the baseline and reached 0.14 at the calibrated operating point; $V_{0,\mathrm{LA}}$ and $V_{0,\mathrm{RA}}$ stayed below 0.01. $C_{\mathrm{SVEN}}$ was therefore held at its scaled value although its influence exceeded the retention threshold. The polynomial chaos expansions had modified leave-one-out errors of 0.043--0.155 across the 25 outputs. All 256 training simulations of the two screens reached the 10-cycle limit before meeting the 1.0~mmHg / 1.0~mL tolerance, so the indices describe outputs from cycles 9--10 of each training simulation.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Principal findings}
This study calibrated a closed-loop lumped-parameter model to the pre-closure catheterization record of one child with a restrictive perimembranous VSD and predicted the post-closure state of the same child without refitting. Five findings follow. First, seven of fourteen candidate parameters governed the nine measured pre-closure targets, and at the scaled baseline the dominant parameter of each target was the determinant that established physiology assigns to it (Table~\ref{tab:physio_gsa}). Second, calibrating those seven reduced the RMSE of relative error over the nine fitted targets from 0.274 to 0.105, and the catheter measurements that were not fitted had relative errors between $-10.8\%$ and $+6.7\%$. Third, the simulated shunt behaved as a restrictive defect: left-to-right throughout the cycle, driven by a 62~mmHg LV--RV gradient and insensitive to pulmonary resistance. Fourth, the simulated response to closure reproduced the direction of the flow changes and of every measured pressure change that the physiology of shunt removal predicts, but not the expected left-heart unloading: LA pressure and LVEDV did not fall (Table~\ref{tab:physio_closure}). Fifth, the post-closure errors fall into three groups: an error carried over from calibration (systolic PA pressure), disagreement between measurement sites and methods (diastolic systemic pressure, $Q_s$), and chamber volumes that no pre-closure measurement constrains.

\subsection{Agreement of the sensitivity structure with established physiology}

\paragraph{The shunt.} In a restrictive defect the orifice limits flow, and the pressure difference between the ventricles, not the ratio of pulmonary to systemic resistance, sets the shunt~\cite{Rao2024,Haas_2024}. The screen reproduces this hierarchy. $Q_p/Q_s$ depended on the discharge coefficient ($S_T = 0.51$), which scales the effective orifice area, and on systemic resistance ($S_T = 0.41$), which sets LV systolic pressure and therefore the driving gradient; pulmonary resistance had $S_T = 0.01$. The simulated LV--RV peak gradient was 62.3~mmHg against 69~mmHg measured, and RV systolic pressure was 30\% of LV systolic pressure, the pressure relation of a restrictive defect. The orifice law of Eq.~(\ref{eq:orifice}) is the same Bernoulli relation clinicians apply to the Doppler jet velocity to derive the VSD gradient and estimate RV systolic pressure~\cite{Uppu2022}, so the calibrated $C_d$ has a direct clinical counterpart. For a nonrestrictive defect the same physiology predicts the opposite hierarchy, with pulmonary resistance dominating $Q_p/Q_s$; the retained parameter set is therefore specific to the defect type and should be re-screened for each patient rather than fixed across a cohort.

\paragraph{Systemic and pulmonary pressures.} Mean and diastolic systemic pressure followed systemic resistance, as the steady pressure--flow relation requires. Systolic systemic pressure depended on resistance ($S_T = 0.43$) more than on arterial compliance ($S_T = 0.12$), whereas pulse pressure physiology gives compliance a larger role in the systolic value~\cite{FloresGeronimo2021}. The narrow sampling range of $C_{\mathrm{SAR}}$ around a value already anchored to the measured stroke volume and pulse pressure explains part of this difference. In the pulmonary circuit the screen separated the steady and pulsatile components of RV afterload exactly as clinical hemodynamics does: pulmonary resistance set the mean and diastolic PA pressure, and PA compliance set the systolic value. Kempton et al.\ showed that PVR and PA compliance computed as stroke volume over pulse pressure together describe global RV afterload~\cite{Kempton2025}, and Gouwy et al.\ showed in children with pulmonary arterial hypertension that PA compliance carries information independent of PVR~\cite{Gouwy2025}. The model's $C_{\mathrm{PAR}}$ seed uses the same stroke-volume-over-pulse-pressure definition (Table~\ref{tab:seeding}). This separation holds at the scaled baseline; at the calibrated operating point, where $C_{\mathrm{PAR}}$ was sampled over a narrower absolute range, pulmonary resistance also dominated systolic PA pressure ($S_T = 0.25$).

\paragraph{Filling pressure and systemic flow.} Mean RA pressure depended on RV passive elastance ($S_T = 0.48$) and, to a lesser extent, on systemic venous compliance, which matches the view that RA pressure reflects both RV filling properties and the mean systemic filling pressure~\cite{Persichini2022}. Systemic flow depended on systemic resistance, pulmonary arterial compliance and systemic venous compliance. In Guyton's framework, cardiac output ($Q_s$) at steady state equals venous return and is set by the mean systemic filling pressure, the RA pressure and the resistance to venous return~\cite{Persichini2022}; the stressed blood volume is the main determinant of the filling pressure. $C_{\mathrm{SVEN}}$, which the code does not calibrate, exceeded the retention threshold for five fitted targets (Section~\ref{sec:results_gsa}), so its influence on RA pressure, PA pressure and $Q_s$ is carried by the calibrated parameters instead. In this model the stressed volume is fixed by the blood-volume constraint of Eq.~(\ref{eq:sven}) and was not among the screened inputs. The screen therefore assigns to arterial resistance part of the control over systemic flow that physiology assigns to venous capacitance and blood volume; this is a property of the chosen input set, not a finding about P1.

\paragraph{Chamber function.} Active elastance controlled end-systolic volume and ejection fraction of both ventricles ($S_T$ 0.59--0.76) and had $S_T < 0.10$ for every measured pressure and flow. This is the separation described by ventricular--arterial coupling: ejection fraction and end-systolic volume reflect contractility relative to arterial load, whereas mean pressures and flows are set mainly by the vascular load and venous return~\cite{Vriz2021,Persichini2022}. The consequence for this study is direct: the pre-closure catheterization contains no quantity that informs contractility, so the chamber parameters were not calibrated, and the post-closure chamber volumes are unconstrained predictions. Lechuga et al.\ found the same split in pulmonary hypertension, where sensitivity screening retained the resistances and elastances that governed measured pressure and flow~\cite{Lechuga2026}, and guidelines for mechanistic cardiovascular modeling recommend exactly this restriction of calibration to the parameters the data inform~\cite{Colebank2024Guidelines,Colunga2023}.

\subsection{Agreement of the simulated pre-closure hemodynamics with the physiology of a restrictive VSD}
The shunt stayed left-to-right throughout the cardiac cycle, with a systolic peak of 0.93~L/min and a diastolic minimum of 0.11~L/min (Figure~\ref{fig:pre_waves}E), because LV pressure exceeded RV pressure at every instant. Of the 4.2~mL shunted per beat, 6\% crossed during isovolumic contraction, 37\% during ejection, 40\% after aortic valve closure while the LV was relaxing, and 17\% during diastolic filling. The LV therefore had no true isovolumic phases before closure (Figure~\ref{fig:pv}), which is the expected mechanics of a ventricle with a second, low-pressure outlet. The split of shunt volume between systole and relaxation depends on the ventricular relaxation duration, which was taken from the adult reference and not calibrated; Doppler recording of the VSD jet over the cycle would test it directly.

The calibrated LVEF of 0.75 before closure is high, and it fell to 0.72 after closure. An LV that ejects part of its stroke volume into the low-pressure RV works against a lower effective afterload, and the loss of that outlet and of the shunt preload after closure is the mechanism proposed for the early fall in LV systolic function after VSD repair~\cite{Safa2023}. The model reproduces the direction of this change.

One pre-closure result does not agree with VSD physiology. A left-to-right VSD loads the left atrium and LV, because the shunted blood returns through the pulmonary veins~\cite{Safa2023,Rao2024}; in a perimembranous restrictive defect the RV is not the volume-loaded chamber. The calibrated model had a larger RV than LV (RVEDV 43.3~mL against LVEDV 39.8~mL), and the measured post-closure volumes show the opposite relation (RVEDV 30.5~mL against LVEDV 36.1~mL). The RV passive elastance, the only chamber parameter calibrated, reached its lower bound: the fit made the RV more compliant to hold mean RA pressure at 5~mmHg, and a compliant RV fills to a large volume. The RV volume is therefore a by-product of fitting RA pressure without a volume measurement, not a representation of the RV of P1.

\paragraph{Where the simulation agrees.} Closing the orifice removed the shunt, pulmonary flow fell by 13\% to equal systemic flow, $Q_p/Q_s$ became 1.00, and mean PA pressure fell by 1.0~mmHg; the measured mean PA pressure fell by 2~mmHg. Mean arterial pressure rose slightly in both the model ($+0.6$~mmHg) and the measurement ($+3.7$~mmHg), RA pressure stayed at about 5~mmHg in both, and LVEF fell. For a shunt of this size ($Q_p/Q_s = 1.19$, 16\% of pulmonary flow), the physiology predicts small hemodynamic changes at closure, and the simulated and measured pressure changes are both small.

\paragraph{Where the simulation does not agree.} Closure of a left-to-right shunt removes part of the pulmonary venous return to the left heart, and LV dimensions in children normalize after transcatheter VSD closure, most of the change occurring in the first month~\cite{Yuan2025}. The model predicted no left-heart unloading: mean LA pressure stayed at 7.9~mmHg and LVEDV rose from 39.8 to 41.1~mL. The cause is visible in the flows. Pulmonary venous return per minute fell by 13\%, but heart rate fell at the same time from 119 to 108~bpm, so pulmonary venous return per beat fell only from 30.6 to 29.4~mL ($-4\%$) while diastole lengthened. With chamber properties held at their pre-closure values, the LV filled to the same or a slightly larger volume. The LV of P1 was 5~mL smaller than predicted one day after closure. Two mechanisms that the model excludes by design can explain the difference: early adaptation of the LV to the acute loss of preload~\cite{Safa2023}, and changes in loading conditions during and after the procedure.

\paragraph{Where the measurement does not agree with physiology.} Two measured changes run against the expected physiology rather than against the model. Systemic flow fell from 3.42 to 2.54~L/min ($-26\%$) and $Q_p/Q_s$ fell below 1.0 after complete closure. Closure does not reduce systemic output, and after complete closure $Q_p/Q_s < 1$ is not possible without a right-to-left pathway, which the procedure did not create. The pre-closure values come from the Fick method and the post-closure values from echocardiography. In pediatric catheterization, Fick output computed with an assumed oxygen consumption has a mean bias of $+27.5\%$ against thermodilution or MRI~\cite{Ralston2023}, and Fick and MRI flows showed poor concordance in children after heart transplantation~\cite{Schramm2023}. A change of the measurement method between the two states is therefore a sufficient explanation for the measured fall in systemic flow and for the rise in derived SVR (19.4 to 27.6~WU), without a change in the circulation of P1. The model, which keeps SVR constant, cannot and should not reproduce that change.

\subsection{Sources of the largest pre-closure errors}
Systolic PA pressure is the dominant error in both states ($z = +5.10$ before and $+8.14$ after closure). The pulmonary resistance scale reached its lower bound and $C_{\mathrm{PAR}}$ stayed near its scaled value, so the model could not narrow the PA pulse pressure from 15.4~mmHg to the measured 10~mmHg while keeping the errors in mean PA pressure and $Q_p/Q_s$ small. A single pulmonary arterial compartment has one compliance for the whole arterial tree, and a narrow pulse pressure with a normal mean is the pattern such a compartment reproduces worst~\cite{Colunga2023,Kempton2025}. A proximal and distal pulmonary arterial compartment is the direct structural remedy.

Systolic RFA pressure was 11.9\% above the model, while the DAo systolic pressure recorded in the same procedure was 3.2\% above it. The model's arterial compartment is proximal, and pulse pressure rises from central to peripheral arteries~\cite{FloresGeronimo2021}; before closure the RFA reading exceeded the DAo reading by 9~mmHg, which is of that kind. After closure the order reversed (DAo 104.3~mmHg, RFA 89~mmHg), so the between-site difference in this record is not consistent across the two states and cannot be attributed to amplification alone. Shunt flow was the third error ($-25.3\%$). It is the difference of two Fick flows, each carrying the assumed-oxygen-consumption error described above~\cite{Ralston2023}; with the declared uncertainties ($\sigma = 0.50$~L/min on $Q_s$, 5\% on $Q_p/Q_s$) the shunt flow has a relative uncertainty of 34\%, so the 25\% error is within one standard deviation.

\subsection{Comparison with related work}
Bozkurt et al.\ optimized 23 parameters of a lumped model for three children with dilated cardiomyopathy and matched pressures, volumes and cardiac output within 10\%~\cite{Bozkurt2022}; every compared quantity in that study was also fitted. Owashi et al.\ estimated LV pressure in twelve patients with aortic stenosis with total relative errors of 11.9\% from invasive data and 12.3\% when only systolic and diastolic arterial pressure were available~\cite{Owashi2020}. Lechuga et al.\ reported $R^2$ between 0.78 and 0.89 for pulmonary pressure waveforms across four phenotypes of pulmonary hypertension~\cite{Lechuga2026}. The pre-closure fit here is of the same order (RMSE of relative error 0.105 over nine targets, pressure RMSE 5.2~mmHg), with fewer measurements and a pediatric shunt lesion. The difference is the post-closure test: those pressures were never fitted, and they had an RMSE of 6.9~mmHg. Harrod et al.\ used lumped-parameter modeling to predict secondary pulmonary hypertension arising from left ventricular diastolic dysfunction~\cite{Harrod2021}, and Kachabi et al.\ inferred pulmonary vascular parameters with quantified uncertainty before and after disease onset~\cite{Kachabi2025}; neither addressed a structural intervention in a child. Colebank et al.\ simulated VSD of three sizes over the first two years of life without calibration to individual patients~\cite{Colebank2026}, and Ferrero et al.\ used a 0D model to interpret catheterization data in intracardiac shunts without predicting a post-intervention state~\cite{Ferrero2026Shunts}. A dedicated post-closure calibration is the natural upper bound on what a forward prediction can achieve, and the gap between that bound and the forward prediction measures how much post-closure information the pre-closure record lacks.

\subsection{Clinical relevance}
The calibrated model gives quantities that the catheterization record does not contain: the shunt flow over the cycle and its split between systole and relaxation, LA pressure, and the pressure--volume loops of both ventricles. Its sensitivity structure matches the physiology clinicians use to reason about a restrictive VSD, so its parameters have a clinical reading: $C_d$ corresponds to the effective defect size behind the Doppler gradient, systemic resistance to LV afterload, and PA compliance to the pulsatile RV load. After closure, the model predicts the direction of every measured catheter pressure change from pre-closure data alone, with relative errors of $+1.2\%$ for systolic and $-6.4\%$ for mean arterial pressure and $+8.2\%$ for RA pressure. It does not predict left-heart unloading or chamber volumes, and it should not be used for those purposes until a pre-closure volume measurement enters calibration and the chamber parameters are allowed to adapt at closure. Clinical decisions on closure rest on defect size, shunt magnitude and symptoms~\cite{Rao2024}; a calibrated model adds a prediction of the post-closure state for the individual child, which is the step towards the cardiovascular digital twins described in recent work~\cite{Niederer2021,Katsoulakis2024}.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item One patient was studied; no claim of population-level performance is made.
\item Two of the seven calibrated parameters rest on their lower bounds, and the reduced $\chi^2$ (15.65 on 2 degrees of freedom) exceeds what the declared uncertainties allow.
\item No chamber volume was measured before closure, so the chamber parameters were not calibrated; the RV volume is a by-product of fitting RA pressure, and the post-closure volumes are unconstrained predictions.
\item Chamber parameters are held fixed at closure, so the model cannot represent acute LV adaptation to the loss of shunt preload.
\item Ventricular activation and relaxation durations come from the adult reference and were not calibrated; the phase split of shunt flow depends on them.
\item The post-closure pressures and volumes were measured one day apart and are treated as one state; pre- and post-closure systemic flow come from different methods (Fick and echocardiography).
\item Two records disagree on the post-closure LV volumes, and the three post-closure measurement sites disagree on diastolic systemic pressure by 16.3~mmHg.
\item The sensitivity screen used 128 training simulations truncated at 10 cycles, which supports ranking but not precise indices, and it did not include blood volume. Sensitivity indices depend on the operating point: the dominant parameter of systolic PA pressure changed between the baseline and the calibrated screen.
\item The venous compliances and atrial unstressed volumes are not calibration candidates in the code; $C_{\mathrm{SVEN}}$ exceeded the retention threshold and was held at its scaled value.
\item No post-closure calibration was performed; the post-closure state is a forward prediction only.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
A closed-loop lumped-parameter model of pediatric VSD was scaled by body weight, screened by global sensitivity analysis, calibrated to one child's pre-closure catheterization, and used without refitting to predict the same child's post-closure state. Calibration reduced the RMSE of relative error over the nine fitted targets from 0.274 to 0.105, and the pre-closure measurements that were not fitted had relative errors between $-10.8\%$ and $+6.7\%$. After closure, the relative errors were $+1.2\%$ for systolic and $-6.4\%$ for mean arterial pressure, $+3.2\%$ for diastolic PA pressure and $+8.2\%$ for RA pressure; the largest were $+40.7\%$ for systolic PA pressure, an error carried over from calibration, and $+42.1\%$ for right-ventricular end-diastolic volume, which no pre-closure measurement constrains. \pending{One sentence on the measurement-noise result.} Adding a pre-closure echocardiographic volume measurement to calibration is the concrete next step this work identifies.

\section{Circulation equations}
\label{app:circuit}
Chamber pressures follow Eq.~(\ref{eq:elastance}); vascular pressures are $P_i = (V_i - V_{0,i})/C_i$ for $i \in \{\mathrm{SAR}, \mathrm{SC}, \mathrm{SVEN}, \mathrm{PAR}, \mathrm{PVEN}\}$, with RA, LA, systemic venous and pulmonary venous pressure bounded below at $-5$~mmHg. Valve flows follow Eq.~(\ref{eq:valve}) and the shunt Eq.~(\ref{eq:orifice}). The systemic capillary resistance is split into two halves around the capillary compliance. The vascular balances are
\begin{align}
\dot{V}_{\mathrm{SAR}} &= Q_{\mathrm{AV}} - Q_{\mathrm{SAR}}, \\
L_{\mathrm{SAR}}\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{SAR}} &= P_{\mathrm{SAR}} - P_{\mathrm{SC}} - \left(R_{\mathrm{SAR}} + \tfrac{1}{2}R_{\mathrm{SC}}\right)Q_{\mathrm{SAR}}, \\
\dot{V}_{\mathrm{SC}} &= Q_{\mathrm{SAR}} - Q_{\mathrm{SC}}, \qquad Q_{\mathrm{SC}} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{SC}} - P_{\mathrm{SVEN}}}{\tfrac{1}{2}R_{\mathrm{SC}}}, \\
\dot{V}_{\mathrm{SVEN}} &= Q_{\mathrm{SC}} - Q_{\mathrm{SVEN}}, \\
L_{\mathrm{SVEN}}\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{SVEN}} &= P_{\mathrm{SVEN}} - P_{\mathrm{RA}} - R_{\mathrm{SVEN}}Q_{\mathrm{SVEN}}, \\
\dot{V}_{\mathrm{PAR}} &= Q_{\mathrm{PV}} - Q_{\mathrm{PAR}}, \\
L_{\mathrm{PAR}}\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{PAR}} &= P_{\mathrm{PAR}} - P_{\mathrm{PC}} - R_{\mathrm{PAR}}Q_{\mathrm{PAR}}, \\
\left(C_{\mathrm{PCOX}} + C_{\mathrm{PCNO}}\right)\dot{P}_{\mathrm{PC}} &= Q_{\mathrm{PAR}} - Q_{\mathrm{PC}}, \qquad Q_{\mathrm{PC}} = \left(P_{\mathrm{PC}} - P_{\mathrm{PVEN}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{R_{\mathrm{PCOX}}} + \frac{1}{R_{\mathrm{PCNO}}}\right), \\
\dot{V}_{\mathrm{PVEN}} &= Q_{\mathrm{PC}} - Q_{\mathrm{PVEN}}, \\
L_{\mathrm{PVEN}}\dot{Q}_{\mathrm{PVEN}} &= P_{\mathrm{PVEN}} - P_{\mathrm{LA}} - R_{\mathrm{PVEN}}Q_{\mathrm{PVEN}}.
\end{align}
